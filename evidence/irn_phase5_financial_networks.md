## Executive Summary

Iran's political economy is structured around three overlapping financial power centers: the traditional bazaari merchant class, the IRGC's sprawling commercial empire, and the clerical foundations (bonyads). These three pillars were initially aligned by the 1979 Revolution but have diverged into a relationship best characterized as **hierarchical cooptation with growing factional tension** — not an integrated elite, and no longer purely cooperative.

The IRGC has emerged as the dominant economic actor, controlling an estimated one-third to two-thirds of GDP through construction conglomerates (Khatam al-Anbiya), investment foundations (Bonyad Taavon Sepah), smuggling monopolies, and sanctions-evasion networks. The bonyads — particularly Bonyad Mostazafan — function as a parallel quasi-state economy answerable only to the Supreme Leader. The traditional bazaari class, once the financial backbone of the Islamic Republic, has been progressively marginalized and, as of the December 2025 Grand Bazaar strikes, appears to be breaking with the regime entirely.

Internationally, Iran has constructed a sophisticated parallel financial architecture: Dubai serves as the primary offshore financial center (8,200+ Iranian-owned companies), hawala networks provide informal value transfer, cryptocurrency mining and trading ($7.8-10B annually) supplements formal channels, and China-Iran oil settlements in yuan through CIPS have replaced dollar-denominated trade. This system is resilient but not invisible — OFAC sanctioned over 875 persons/entities in 2025 alone targeting these networks.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Three-Pillar Economy: Bazaari, IRGC, Bonyads

#### The Traditional Bazaari
The bazaari merchant class historically operated as the financial engine of Iranian society and a critical political constituency. Tehran's Grand Bazaar — 10+ kilometers of corridors — functioned as a combined marketplace, banking center, gold exchange, and currency trading hub. The bazaari-clerical alliance was foundational to the 1979 Revolution, with bazaar merchants providing both funding and organizational infrastructure.

The bazaari class operates through traditional commercial mechanisms: commodity trading, gold dealing, currency exchange (sarrafi shops regulated by Bank Markazi), textile and carpet exports, and import-export businesses. Their international networks extend through kinship and ethnic ties to Dubai, Turkey, Iraq, Pakistan, Afghanistan, and Central Asia.

#### IRGC Economic Empire
The IRGC's transformation from a military force into an economic conglomerate began with the end of the Iran-Iraq War (1988) and accelerated through the 1990s reconstruction period. The scale is staggering:

- **Khatam al-Anbiya Construction HQ** (est. 1989): 812+ registered subsidiary companies, 1,700+ government contracts (as of 2012), 55,000 employees including 40,000 military and 15,000 Basij, official revenue of $12 billion, operating in roads, tunnels, dams, pipelines, airports, metro systems, oil/gas, and telecommunications. Designated by OFAC in 2006.
- **Bonyad Taavon Sepah** (IRGC Cooperative Foundation, est. 1989): Iran's fifth-largest holding company, operating in construction, agriculture, energy (Kermanshah Petrochemical), telecom, banking (Ansar Bank — through which all IRGC salaries are paid), and automotive (Bahman Group, Saipa). Sanctioned by US Treasury since 2010.
- **Smuggling operations**: The IRGC controls Iran's borders and maintains a monopoly on smuggling through ports and airports under its direct control. Products include consumer goods, alcohol, narcotics, weapons, and tobacco. This is not incidental — it is a structured revenue source.
- **GDP share**: Estimates range from 10% to over 50%, with the most credible assessments placing IRGC and affiliated foundations at roughly one-third of GDP, or "tens of billions of dollars."

#### Bonyad System
The bonyads are parastatal foundations created from properties confiscated after the 1979 Revolution. They operate with exemptions from taxes, antitrust laws, and government oversight, answerable only to the Supreme Leader.

- **Bonyad Mostazafan** (Foundation for the Oppressed): Iran's second-largest commercial enterprise after NIOC and the largest holding company in the Middle East. Controls 140+ factories, 470 agricultural companies, 100 construction firms, 64 mines, 250 commercial companies. Revenue approximately $6.6 billion (FY2017). Estimated at 1-10% of GDP depending on methodology.
- **Total bonyad sector**: Controls an estimated 20-30% of Iran's non-oil economy.
- Bonyads and IRGC share personnel through cross-appointment of IRGC officers to bonyad directorships.

### 2. Integrated Elite or Competing Factions?

**Assessment: Hierarchical cooptation with growing fractures.**

Evidence for integration:
- IRGC officers hold bonyad directorships, creating institutional overlap
- Both IRGC and bonyads answer to the Supreme Leader, who appoints their leadership
- Shared interest in regime survival and sanctions evasion
- Personnel rotation between military, bonyad, and state economic posts

Evidence for competition:
- The bazaari class has been systematically marginalized by IRGC and bonyad economic expansion
- Private contractors cannot compete with IRGC firms that enjoy conscript labor, military equipment, state funding, and tax exemptions
- Ahmadinejad-era "privatization" transferred state assets not to private sector but to IRGC/bonyad entities, creating resentment
- The December 2025 Grand Bazaar strike — the first time the bazaari class has broken with the regime on this scale since the Revolution — was explicitly driven by IRGC economic dominance
- The IRGC's smuggling monopoly directly undercuts legitimate bazaari import-export businesses

The trajectory is clear: the bazaari have been reduced from alliance partners to subcontractors, and by late 2025, to open opposition. The IRGC-bonyad nexus has effectively captured the state economy, creating what Clingendael Institute terms a "military-bonyad complex" accounting for over 50% of GDP.

### 3. Dubai as Iran's Offshore Financial Center

Dubai is the single most important node in Iran's international financial architecture:

- **8,200+ Iranian-owned companies** operate in the UAE, concentrated in Dubai
- **300,000+ Iranian residents** in the UAE, primarily in Dubai
- **Jebel Ali Free Zone** (JAFZA): Offers zero corporate tax, 100% foreign ownership, no currency restrictions. The Wisconsin Project identified 20+ Iranian companies operating specifically in JAFZA. Goods are re-exported through Dubai, with Iran and India as the primary destinations.
- **Trade volume**: UAE accounted for $4 billion of Iran's non-oil exports (10.5% of total)
- **Gold corridor**: Gold shipments via JAFZA masked as electronics have been documented matching Iranian export spikes post-sanctions. FinCEN advisories indicate UAE hubs process approximately 15% of global sanctions-evasion USD flows, with gold comprising 40% of DMCC's evasion share.
- **134 UAE companies** identified by independent researchers as systematically facilitating sanctions evasion for Iran and Russia through shell companies, nominee directors, falsified trade documents, vessel-to-vessel transfers, and AIS manipulation.

As of March 2026, the UAE is considering restricting Iranian access to billions of dollars held in Dubai financial networks — a decision that would represent a seismic shift in the Iran-Dubai financial relationship.

### 4. Hawala and Informal Value Transfer

Hawala networks are the connective tissue between Iran's domestic bazaari economy and international trade:

- Traditional bazaari merchants are directly linked to the regional hawala hub in Dubai
- Iranian businessmen use hawala with counterparties in the UK, Turkey, and Dubai, applying transaction thresholds to avoid triggering reporting requirements
- Hawala provides value transfer without moving money across borders — brokers settle through trade, cash, and net settlement over extended periods
- The system predates and coexists with formal banking, but has become critical as sanctions have severed Iran's access to SWIFT and correspondent banking

FATF has maintained Iran as a "high-risk jurisdiction" with "significant strategic deficiencies" since 2020, calling for enhanced due diligence and countermeasures. Hardline elements in Tehran have resisted FATF compliance specifically because it would expose funding channels to Hezbollah and other proxies.

### 5. Iranian Banking System and Global Finance

Iran's banking system has been systematically disconnected from global finance:

- **March 2012**: SWIFT disconnected all EU-sanctioned Iranian banks — Bank Melli, Bank Saderat, Bank Tejarat, and Bank Markazi (Central Bank of Iran). This was described as "crippling."
- **January 2016**: JCPOA implementation led to reconnection of many Iranian banks to SWIFT.
- **November 2018**: Following US withdrawal from JCPOA, SWIFT again suspended Iranian bank access.
- **Current status**: Iranian banks remain largely disconnected from the global SWIFT network, forcing reliance on alternative settlement mechanisms.

Bank Markazi (Central Bank) sets monetary policy and regulates the sarrafi (exchange) shops, but the domestic banking system is described as "paralyzed by opaque and outdated structures." The formal banking sector has become a shell — real financial activity flows through IRGC channels, bonyad networks, hawala, and cryptocurrency.

### 6. Tehran Grand Bazaar as Financial Hub

The Grand Bazaar functions as a combined marketplace and financial center:
- Gold and jewelry trading concentrated at Sabzeh Meidan on 15th Khordad Street
- Sarrafi (currency exchange) shops operate under Central Bank regulation but at market-determined rates
- The "free market" rate for the rial is published independently (e.g., bonbast.com) and diverges significantly from official rates
- The bazaar historically provided the informal credit infrastructure for Iranian commerce — merchant-to-merchant lending, letters of credit, and trade finance outside the formal banking system
- This function has been partially displaced by IRGC-controlled financial channels but remains active for domestic commerce

### 7. International Trade Settlement Mechanisms

Iran has developed multiple parallel settlement channels:

**Yuan/CIPS settlement**: 80-90% of Iranian crude exports to China settle in yuan or barter through CIPS (China International Payments System), which processed 175 trillion yuan in 2025 (43% YoY increase). China purchases approximately 90% of Iran's crude oil exports.

**Cryptocurrency**: Iran legalized crypto mining and required licensed miners to sell mined Bitcoin directly to Bank Markazi for import payments. TRM Labs estimated $10 billion in Iran-linked crypto activity in 2025; Chainalysis reported $7.8 billion received by Iranian wallets. In 2025, Iran became the first known nation-state to publicly accept cryptocurrency for weapons exports.

**Barter trade**: Oil-for-goods arrangements, particularly with China. Gold is used as hard currency in transactions that cannot be denominated in dollars.

**Shadow fleet**: 29+ vessels identified by OFAC transporting Iranian petroleum using AIS shutdown, ship-to-ship transfers, and flag manipulation.

**BRICS mechanisms**: Iran joined BRICS in 2024. Project mBridge (multi-CBDC platform involving China, UAE, Saudi Arabia, and others) reached minimum viable product in 2024 with $55 billion in transaction volume by early 2026.

### 8. The "Resistance Economy"

Ayatollah Khamenei introduced the concept of "Eghtesad-e Moghavemati" (Resistance Economy) in 2011 as a policy framework for sanctions resilience:

**Stated goals**: Import substitution, technology indigenization, reduced dependency on imports, local production prioritization.

**Actual achievements**: Near self-sufficiency in pharmaceuticals and home appliances. Domestic manufacturing covers food, textiles, automobiles, though without global competitiveness.

**Gap between rhetoric and reality**: Academic analysis (Batmanghelidj, JHU) finds that "the political rhetoric of resistance, which posits responding to sanctions with self-sufficiency, does not reflect the reality of Iran's economic response." Iran's economy is more open to global trade than ever — it simply routes through different channels (China, UAE, Turkey, Central Asia) rather than Western financial infrastructure.

The resistance economy is better understood as a **parallel financial architecture** than as autarky. Iran has not withdrawn from global trade — it has built alternative plumbing.

### 9. Iran's Parallel System: Threat or Component?

Iran's financial architecture is neither fully autonomous nor fully integrated with Western systems. It operates as a **parasitic parallel system** — one that depends on gaps, corruption, and willing intermediaries within the Western-dominated architecture:

- Dubai's free zones are creations of Western-aligned financial regulation
- CIPS exists because of the dollar system's dominance
- Cryptocurrency was invented and largely operates within Western technological infrastructure
- Hawala networks interface with formal banking at both ends
- Iran's shadow fleet uses the same shipping infrastructure as legitimate trade

The system is a threat insofar as it demonstrates the limits of sanctions as a coercive tool — Iran has maintained economic function despite the most comprehensive sanctions regime in history. It is a component insofar as it could not exist without the global system it seeks to circumvent. The $94 billion processed through a single sanctioned digital exchange (Zedcex) suggests the scale of financial flows that continue despite formal prohibition.

## Negative Findings

1. **No evidence of a unified "Iran Inc." command structure.** Despite overlapping personnel and shared loyalty to the Supreme Leader, the IRGC, bonyads, and bazaari class do not operate as a coordinated economic entity. Competition for contracts, market share, and political influence is real and intensifying.

2. **Bazaari-IRGC alliance narrative is obsolete.** The claim that the bazaari class remains a regime pillar was contradicted by the December 2025 Grand Bazaar strikes — the most significant bazaari political action against the regime since 1979.

3. **"Resistance economy" as self-sufficiency is largely rhetorical.** Academic analysis consistently finds that Iran's economy is more globally integrated (through alternative channels) than the autarky rhetoric suggests. The "resistance" is in the routing, not in withdrawal from trade.

4. **FATF compliance resistance is not irrational.** Hardline opposition to FATF standards reflects a calculated assessment that compliance would expose Hezbollah funding channels and sanctioned project financing — not mere bureaucratic resistance.

5. **Cryptocurrency as complete sanctions bypass — OVERSTATED.** While significant ($7.8-10B), Iran-linked crypto represents a fraction of total trade. The most critical settlement mechanism remains yuan-denominated oil trade through CIPS, not crypto.

6. **No evidence that hawala networks are centrally directed by the Iranian state.** Hawala operates through decentralized merchant networks based on kinship and trust. The state benefits from but does not control these networks. IRGC involvement is at the port/border control level, not at the hawala broker level.

7. **Mike Benz claims about Bear Stearns "$13B clearing" through Iranian channels — ENTIRELY UNSOURCED (cross-ref PKG-EP Phase 3, BSB claims).** This claim circulates in alternative media but has no documentary basis.
