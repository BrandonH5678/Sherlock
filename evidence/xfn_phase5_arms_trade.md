## Executive Summary

The global arms trade operates as a **hybrid market-cartel system** — neither a pure competitive market nor a centrally coordinated supply chain. The structural finding of this analysis is that cross-faction arms supply is not an anomaly but a **systemic feature** of the international defense industry, operating through at least four distinct mechanisms:

1. **Component leakage** — Western dual-use technology (semiconductors, navigation systems) reaches adversaries through third-country transshipment networks despite sanctions and export controls. This is the dominant mechanism by volume.
2. **State-level diversion** — Weapons sold legally to allied governments (Saudi Arabia, UAE) are diverted to embargoed conflict zones (Yemen, Libya, Sudan). This is documented at E1 by UN Panels of Experts.
3. **Multi-client export strategies** — Companies and states (Turkey/Baykar, Israel/Elbit) sell to customers who are rivals or adversaries of each other, without formal restriction. This is legal and openly practiced.
4. **Illicit broker networks** — Individual arms dealers (historically Viktor Bout, currently diffuse networks) deliberately supply opposing factions. This mechanism has been partially disrupted but replaced by decentralized crypto-hawala financing.

The arms trade's cross-faction character is **structural, not conspiratorial**. It emerges from the intersection of profit incentives, weak enforcement of end-user certificates, third-country transshipment, and the dual-use nature of most weapons components. The closest analogue to BCCI's role in arms finance today is a **diffuse ecosystem** of cryptocurrency wallets, hawala networks, and front companies — no single institution has replaced BCCI, but the functional capacity has been replicated across multiple channels.

**Critical finding:** The four largest US semiconductor companies (AMD, Analog Devices, Intel, Texas Instruments) had their components found in Russian weapons used against Ukraine — including components manufactured *after* sanctions were imposed. The US Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations (September 2024) found that none of the four companies had fully compliant export control programs, and exports to suspected transshipment countries (Kazakhstan, Turkey, Armenia) increased by up to 1,000x post-invasion.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Global Arms Industry: Scale and Concentration

The SIPRI Top 100 arms-producing companies recorded **$679 billion** in combined arms revenues in 2024, a 5.9% increase over 2023 and the highest level ever recorded. The industry is dominated by US firms:

| 1 | Lockheed Martin | USA | 64.65 | ~89% |
| 2 | RTX Corporation | USA | 43.60 | ~66% |

| 5 | General Dynamics | USA | 33.63 | ~70% |
| 6 | Boeing | USA | 30.55 | ~46% |
| 7-10 | Various (L3Harris, Leonardo, Thales, AVIC) | Mixed | Variable | Variable |

The five largest suppliers of major arms (USA, France, Russia, China, Germany) account for **71%** of global arms exports (SIPRI 2020-24 data). The USA alone holds a **43% share** of global arms exports.

### 2. Western Components in Russian Weapons (Ukraine Case Study)

This is the most comprehensively documented case of cross-faction technology flow in contemporary conflict.

**RUSI "Silicon Lifeline" Report (August 2022):** Analyzed 27 Russian weapons systems and found **450+ foreign-made components**. US companies manufactured 208 components; European companies manufactured at least 55. Systems analyzed ranged from cruise missiles to air defense to electronic warfare.

**Conflict Armament Research (2022-2025):** Working directly with Ukrainian military, CAR documented Western components in downed Russian weapons. Key finding: some components were manufactured *after* sanctions were imposed (post-February 2022), proving active sanctions evasion, not pre-war stockpiles.

**US Senate Report (September 2024):** Four US semiconductor companies — AMD, Analog Devices, Intel, and Texas Instruments — are the most prevalent in Russian weapons. These four companies:
- Significantly increased exports to transshipment countries post-invasion
- Exports to Kazakhstan rose nearly **1,000x** from 2021 to 2022
- None had fully BIS-compliant export control programs
- BIS had not reviewed any of their compliance programs in 2+ years post-invasion

**Iranian Shahed-136 drones (Russian-manufactured variant):** CAR found **500+ different components**, with **82%** made by US-based companies. These drones are manufactured in Russia using Iranian design and Western electronics, then used against Ukrainian civilian infrastructure.

**Transshipment network:** China, Turkey, Kazakhstan, Armenia, Georgia, and Uzbekistan serve as primary transshipment nodes. The US Treasury sanctioned 275 individuals and entities in October 2024 across 17 jurisdictions. Turkish companies (Bosphorus Gate, Globus Turkey) were specifically named as semiconductor pipeline conduits.

### 3. State-Level Arms Diversion: Saudi Arabia and UAE

**Saudi Arabia → Yemen:**
- UN Panel of Experts on Yemen has documented Saudi and UAE re-export of US-manufactured armored vehicles to non-state armed groups
- US/UK/French weapons sold to the Saudi-led coalition under legal arms transfers have been documented reaching conflict zones
- BAE Systems sold **£17.6 billion** in aircraft, weapons, and services to Saudi Arabia during the Yemen war (2015-present)
- UK Court of Appeal ruled (June 2019) that UK arms sales to Saudi Arabia for use in Yemen were **unlawful**

**UAE → Sudan/Libya:**
- **Sudan (2023-present):** UN experts cited "credible allegations" of large UAE weapons transfers to the RSF (Rapid Support Forces) via Chad. Cargo planes documented at Amdjarass airstrip. Chinese Norinco 155mm AH-4 howitzers — sold only to the UAE — seized from RSF positions in Khartoum (March 2025). The UAE is the only documented importer of this system.
- **Libya:** Multiple UN Panel of Experts reports documented UAE arms deliveries to Haftar's forces. Al-Khadim Airbase east of Benghazi equipped with Chinese Wing Loong II drones operated by Emirati teams since 2016.
- **The International Golden Group pipeline:** FRANCE 24 investigation documented Bulgarian-manufactured mortar shells (Dunarit company) sold to International Golden Group (UAE), then diverted to Sudan via eastern Libya. 105,000 mortar bombs in a single 2019 transaction represented over half of Bulgaria's arms exports to the UAE. International Golden Group was acquired by Edge Group (UAE state-owned defense conglomerate) in January 2024 — nationalizing the diversion infrastructure.
- **Amnesty International (May 2025):** Advanced Chinese weaponry provided by UAE identified in breach of Sudan arms embargo.

### 4. Turkey: Multi-Client Drone Exports

Turkey (Baykar) has sold Bayraktar TB2 drones to **34+ countries**, including to parties on opposing sides of conflicts:

| Buyer | Adversary/Rival Also Buying Turkish Arms | Conflict Context |
|-------|------------------------------------------|-----------------|
| Ukraine | Russia (NATO ally Turkey maintains relations with both) | Russo-Ukrainian War |
| Azerbaijan | Armenia (Russian ally) | Nagorno-Karabakh |
| Ethiopia | (Multiple parties in Tigray conflict) | Ethiopian Civil War |
| Libya GNA | Libya LNA (UAE-backed, also Turkish customer in other domains) | Libyan Civil War |
| Morocco | (Regional rival Algeria buys Russian/Chinese) | North Africa |
| Niger | (Opposing factions in Sahel) | Sahel instability |

Turkey attaches **no political conditions** to drone exports, unlike US practice. This is a deliberate strategic choice — Turkey uses arms exports as an instrument of multi-vector foreign policy, maintaining relationships with all sides. The US expressed "profound humanitarian concerns" over TB2 sales to Ethiopia (December 2021) but took no enforcement action.

Baykar built a factory in Ukraine, deepening the Ukraine relationship, while Turkey simultaneously purchased Russian S-400 air defense systems — the clearest example of a NATO member operating cross-faction arms relationships at the state level.

### 5. Israeli Defense Exports: Strategic Ambiguity

Israeli defense exports reached a record **$14.7-14.8 billion** in 2024, the fourth consecutive record year. Israel sells to countries that are rivals or adversaries of each other:

- **India and Pakistan** are both Israeli defense customers (India is the far larger buyer; Pakistan relationship is limited/historic)
- **Azerbaijan** ($300M+ Hermes 900 order) and **Armenia** are on opposing sides
- **Morocco** ($229M Elbit ATMOS howitzers) and **Algeria** are regional rivals
- **UAE, Bahrain, Morocco** (Abraham Accords partners: 12% of exports, $1.8B) while also maintaining defense relationships that serve anti-Iran coalition building
- Israel **blocked** Pegasus spyware sales to Ukraine and Estonia to avoid antagonizing Russia — using arms sales as diplomatic leverage in multiple directions

**NSO Group/Pegasus:** Israel used cyber-weapon exports as explicit diplomatic currency. Sales were approved/blocked based on foreign policy calculations, not just security assessment. Sales to Saudi Arabia and UAE served Abraham Accords diplomacy. Sales to autocratic governments (Morocco, Rwanda, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain) were approved despite documented use against journalists and dissidents.

### 6. BAE Systems: The Quintessential Cross-Faction Company

BAE Systems (UK, ranked #4 globally, $33.79B arms revenue) operates the most geographically diverse cross-faction portfolio of any major defense company:

- **Al-Yamamah deal (Saudi Arabia):** At least £43 billion since 1985. SFO investigation uncovered up to £6 billion in "commission" payments (bribes) to Saudi royals, including £1 billion+ to Prince Bandar bin Sultan. Investigation shut down by UK government on "national security" grounds (2006).
- **BAE Systems Inc.** is one of the six largest US DoD suppliers — meaning BAE simultaneously serves US and Saudi defense needs
- Parallel corruption investigations in South Africa, Chile, Czech Republic, Romania, Tanzania, and Qatar
- Surveillance technology sales to **50+ countries**, often channeled through non-UK subsidiaries to avoid UK export licensing
- **Venezuela subsidiary** confirmed — demonstrating willingness to operate across geopolitical lines

BAE's structure — with subsidiaries in multiple jurisdictions each subject to different export control regimes — is the corporate architecture of cross-faction supply. The parent company can sell to Saudi Arabia under UK rules while the US subsidiary sells to the Pentagon, and non-UK subsidiaries sell surveillance tech to countries that neither UK nor US would directly approve.

### 7. Viktor Bout and the Cross-Faction Arms Dealer Model

Viktor Bout ("Merchant of Death") represents the purest historical case of deliberate cross-faction supply:

- **Angola:** Supplied both the government AND UNITA rebels simultaneously
- **Afghanistan:** Sold to both the Taliban AND the Northern Alliance
- **DRC:** Supplied both sides of the civil war
- **Additional clients:** Liberia (Charles Taylor), Sierra Leone, Rwanda, Sudan, Philippines

Bout operated the world's largest private fleet of Antonov cargo planes capable of heavy airlift into austere environments. He was convicted in 2011, released in December 2022 prisoner swap (for Brittney Griner), and reportedly returned to arms dealing by 2024 (Al Jazeera reporting, October 2024).

**Structural significance:** Bout demonstrates that cross-faction supply is not only a systemic byproduct of legal arms markets but also a deliberate business model for illicit dealers. The profit incentive is maximal when selling to all parties in a conflict.

### 8. Arms Trade Finance: Post-BCCI Architecture

BCCI served as the primary financial infrastructure for covert arms deals from the early 1980s until its shutdown in 1991. The Kerry-Brown Senate report (1992) documented BCCI's criminality as encompassing "arms trafficking" alongside money laundering, terrorism support, nuclear proliferation, and sex trafficking (E1 — Congressional record, already in Sherlock database).

**No single institution has replaced BCCI.** Instead, the arms trade financing function has been disaggregated across multiple channels:

**Cryptocurrency:**
- OFAC (April 2025) sanctioned a Houthi weapons procurement network using crypto to purchase weapons from Russia. Eight crypto wallets identified moved **nearly $1 billion** in illicit funds.
- Iran accepts cryptocurrency for military equipment purchases
- The Houthi-Iran-Russia procurement triangle uses crypto to complement traditional payment methods

**Hawala Networks:**
- "Hawala 2.0" integrates cryptocurrency, encrypted platforms, and digital assets
- Yemen-Somalia arms trade financed through hawala operators with inadequate AML/KYC controls
- FATF has documented hawala's role in arms trafficking and terrorism financing

**Front Companies and Shell Networks:**
- UAE's International Golden Group → acquired by state-owned Edge Group (2024)
- Turkish transshipment companies (Bosphorus Gate, Globus Turkey)
- Central Asian intermediaries (Uzstanex, Kazstanex) for Russian sanctions evasion
- Hong Kong as transshipment hub for dual-use technology

**The functional replacement for BCCI is not a bank but an ecosystem:** the combination of cryptocurrency, hawala, front companies, and state-adjacent entities (like Edge Group/IGG) collectively provides the same services BCCI offered — anonymous cross-border transfers, embargo circumvention, and plausible deniability.

### 9. End-User Certificate Failure

The legal framework intended to prevent arms diversion — End-User Certificates (EUCs) — is structurally inadequate:

- EUCs can be **easily forged** and are **poorly scrutinized** (SIPRI assessment)
- Three categories of failure: forgery, false/misleading information, unauthorized post-delivery diversion
- The Wassenaar Arrangement (42 member states) provides the multilateral framework, but enforcement is national and inconsistent
- **Dual-use technology** — items with both civilian and military applications — falls into a regulatory gap where export licenses depend on "end use" declarations that are routinely falsified

### 10. The Structural Question: Market or Coordination?

**Finding: The arms trade is a market, not a conspiracy — but the market has structural features that produce cross-faction supply as a predictable outcome.**

The evidence supports a **market with cartel features** model:
- The top 5 exporters control 71% of the market (oligopoly)
- Government-to-government sales create political dependencies that override commercial logic
- End-user certificate systems provide the appearance of control without effective enforcement
- Dual-use technology flows through commercial channels that are inherently difficult to restrict
- States (Turkey, Israel, UAE) deliberately use arms sales as multi-vector diplomatic instruments, which necessarily involves selling to rivals

**What is NOT supported:** There is no evidence of a single coordinating body directing cross-faction supply. The pattern is emergent, not orchestrated. Each mechanism (component leakage, state diversion, multi-client exports, illicit brokering) operates through its own logic, with different actors, motives, and enforcement gaps.

## Negative Findings

1. **No evidence of a coordinated "sell to both sides" conspiracy among top defense contractors.** Cross-faction supply is an emergent market phenomenon, not a directed program. Lockheed Martin, RTX, Northrop Grumman, and General Dynamics sell overwhelmingly to US allies through government-to-government FMS channels. Their components reach adversaries through supply chain failures, not deliberate policy. CONFIDENCE: 0.80.

2. **No direct evidence that top US defense contractors knowingly sell to adversarial nations through subsidiaries.** Licensed production agreements (F-16 in Turkey, South Korea, etc.) are with US allies, not adversaries. The cross-faction flow occurs downstream through component diversion, not at the prime contractor level. CONFIDENCE: 0.75.

3. **The BCCI-arms trade link is already well-documented in Sherlock (PKG-EP).** This report does not duplicate those findings. The new finding is the *absence* of a successor institution — the post-BCCI landscape is diffuse, not centralized. CONFIDENCE: 0.80.

4. **No confirmed evidence that India and Pakistan simultaneously purchase weapons from the same Israeli companies in active competition.** Israel's Pakistan defense relationship is historic/minimal. India is the major buyer. This is not a symmetric cross-faction supply case. CONFIDENCE: 0.70.

5. **Viktor Bout's reported return to arms dealing (2024) is single-source (Al Jazeera) and should be treated as E2 until further corroboration.** CONFIDENCE: 0.60.

6. **Adani-Elbit JV imports from Russia ($70M since Jan 2024) are defense parts, not complete weapons systems.** The cross-faction dimension (India buying from both Russia and Israel) operates at the state level, not at the Adani corporate level per se. CONFIDENCE: 0.70.

7. **Turkey's S-400 purchase from Russia while being a NATO member and Bayraktar TB2 supplier to Ukraine does NOT indicate coordination.** It indicates a deliberate multi-vector foreign policy in which arms trade serves diplomatic positioning. CONFIDENCE: 0.80.

## Analytical Assessment: Structural Architecture of Cross-Faction Arms Supply

The cross-faction arms trade operates through a **four-layer architecture:**

**Layer 1 — Legal Government-to-Government Sales:** The $679B/year legal arms industry. Governments sell to allies. Cross-faction supply at this layer is rare and typically involves states with multi-vector foreign policies (Turkey, Israel, France). This layer generates the largest revenue but the least cross-faction flow by volume.

**Layer 2 — Diversion After Legal Sale:** Weapons legally sold to Saudi Arabia, UAE, or other buyers are diverted to embargoed destinations. This is the most documented layer (UN Panel of Experts reports, Amnesty International, CAR). The UAE-Sudan-Libya pipeline and Saudi-Yemen diversion are the clearest current examples. The International Golden Group/Edge Group case shows state-level institutionalization of diversion.

**Layer 3 — Dual-Use Component Leakage:** The $1B+ annual flow of Western semiconductors to Russia through transshipment countries. This is the layer with the greatest impact on current conflict outcomes (Russian weapons capability depends on these components). Enforcement failure is documented and systemic.

**Layer 4 — Illicit Brokering and Finance:** The Bout model (individual dealers), crypto-hawala networks (Houthi procurement), and state-adjacent front companies. This layer handles the most sensitive transactions — embargoed weapons to non-state actors, nuclear/WMD components, arms to terrorist organizations.

Each layer has its own enforcement gap, its own set of actors, and its own financial infrastructure. The overall system is **resilient precisely because it is decentralized** — disrupting any single layer (shutting down BCCI, convicting Bout, sanctioning transshipment companies) does not eliminate cross-faction supply because the other layers continue operating.
