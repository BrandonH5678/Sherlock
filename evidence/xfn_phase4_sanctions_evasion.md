## Executive Summary

Modern sanctions evasion operates as a multi-layered, industrialized infrastructure that renders sanctions regimes substantially porous. The evidence reveals three tiers of evasion: (1) state-coordinated systems (Russia's shadow fleet, Iran's hawala networks, China's CIPS expansion), (2) opportunistic intermediary jurisdictions (UAE, Turkey, India, Singapore, Malaysia) that profit from regulatory arbitrage, and (3) systemic Western complicity through attestation regimes that are not designed to be verified. The key analytical finding is that sanctions function less as hard barriers and more as a tax on sanctioned trade -- raising transaction costs 15-30% while allowing the vast majority of trade to continue through alternative channels. FinCEN identified $9 billion in Iranian shadow banking through US correspondent accounts in a single year (2024), suggesting the US financial system itself is a conduit. The $60/bbl Russian oil price cap was systematically violated on ESPO crude from day one, with enforcement relying on self-attestation. The shadow fleet has grown to 1,900+ vessels. India refined $14.3 billion in Russian-origin petroleum products and exported them to the EU in FY2024-25. The historical parallel to Iraq's Oil-for-Food scandal -- where 2,200 companies in 66 countries paid $1.8 billion in kickbacks -- suggests sanctions evasion is a structural feature of sanctions regimes, not an aberration.

The central analytical question -- whether sanctions are a real policy tool or managed theater -- resolves to: **they are both**. Sanctions create real friction (Russia's GDP contraction, Iran's currency collapse) while simultaneously generating enormous profit opportunities for intermediary actors. The enforcement gap is not a bug; it is the equilibrium point where Western governments can claim action while maintaining energy supply and trade relationships they depend on. Confidence: 0.65-0.75.

## Key Findings

### 1. Russian Energy Sanctions Evasion Architecture

Russia has constructed the most sophisticated state-coordinated sanctions evasion system in modern history. The architecture consists of:

**Shadow Fleet (Dark Fleet):** 1,900+ vessels as of Q3 2025, up from approximately 200 pre-2022. Of tankers loaded with sanctioned Russian oil, 59% are sanctioned vessels and another 17% are "shadow fleet" vessels with opaque ownership structures. The fleet employs AIS spoofing (6x more extended outages than European-flagged vessels), ship-to-ship transfers at designated hotspots (Laconian Gulf, Hurd's Bank near Malta, waters off Ceuta, Gulf of Oman, Malaysian waters), false port calls, relay voyages, and frequent flag changes. Approximately 70 dark fleet tankers reregistered under Russian flag since May 2025.

**India Refining Pipeline:** India's exports of refined fuels (diesel, jet fuel) to the EU jumped 58% in the first three quarters of 2024. In FY2024-25, India exported approximately $14.3 billion in petroleum products to the EU, most refined from discounted Russian crude. The Netherlands functions as the distribution hub. Nayara Energy (49% Rosneft-owned, Vadinar refinery, Gujarat) processed predominantly Russian crude and exported 3 MMt of refined fuel in H1 2025 before the EU's 18th sanctions package designated it in July 2025. Reliance Industries' Jamnagar complex (world's largest refinery) is also a major Russian crude client.

**Turkey Refining Pipeline:** Tupras imported a record 225,000 bpd of Russian crude in 2024 -- the highest ever. SOCAR's STAR refinery also processed Russian crude. However, Turkey's refiners began shifting to non-Russian sources in late 2025 after the January 2025 US sanctions on Rosneft and Lukoil, suggesting secondary sanctions create more compliance pressure than primary sanctions on intermediary jurisdictions.

**Price Cap Evasion:** The $60/bbl price cap relies on attestation from market participants that is "not systematically verified" (OFAC's own language). Virtually all ESPO crude exports from Pacific ports traded above the cap from inception. The attestation system was tightened to per-voyage (from annual) in February 2024, and the EU proposed lowering the cap to $47.60 in its 18th sanctions package (July 2025), implicitly acknowledging the original cap was too generous to be effective.

**Swiss Trading Hub:** Geneva-based Litasco (Lukoil's trading arm) was once Russia's biggest oil trader, rivaling Trafigura and Vitol. US sanctions (effective November 2025) dismantled Litasco's operations -- most traders dismissed. However, the broader Swiss commodity trading infrastructure (Trafigura, Vitol, Glencore) that facilitated pre-sanctions Russian oil trade has adapted rather than exited.

### 2. Iranian Sanctions Evasion Architecture

Iran's evasion system is older, more decentralized, and relies heavily on informal finance:

**Shadow Banking:** FinCEN identified approximately $9 billion in potential Iranian shadow banking activity flowing through US correspondent accounts in 2024. This is a primary-source finding (E1) that demonstrates the US banking system itself is penetrated by Iranian evasion networks.

**Front Company Networks:** Iranian operatives establish front companies in UAE free trade zones and Turkey. Four Dubai-based front companies supplied $800 million in cash to Ansar Exchange. OFAC sanctioned 39 entities and 25 individuals across Iran, Turkey, UAE, Hong Kong in June 2024 targeting these networks.

**Hawala Networks:** Informal value transfer systems operating through trusted brokers who settle accounts through trade or cash deliveries. These bypass formal banking entirely and are extremely difficult to track.

**Iraq Dollar Auctions:** Iran-linked exchangers exploited Iraq's Central Bank dollar auctions to extract hard currency. US crackdown on Iraqi banks in 2023 only partially disrupted this channel. Treasury designated Iraqi individuals and entities laundering tens of millions for IRGC-Quds Force.

**Oil Laundering:** Iranian oil disguised as Iraqi exports shipped to China. This is a well-documented pattern designated in OFAC actions.

### 3. Chinese Capital Flow Mechanisms

China facilitates sanctions evasion through both institutional infrastructure and active policy tolerance:

**CIPS Growth:** Cross-Border Interbank Payment System processed 8.22 million transactions totaling RMB 175.49 trillion ($24.47 trillion) in 2024, up 42.6% year-on-year. 190 direct participants and 1,567 indirect participants across 124 countries (as of November 2025). However, approximately 80% of CIPS payments still use SWIFT messaging (Yeung and Goh, 2022), meaning CIPS is not yet an independent sanctions evasion infrastructure.

**US-China Economic and Security Review Commission (November 2025):** Found that China is actively facilitating Russia, Iran, and North Korea's ability to evade sanctions through "increasingly sophisticated mechanisms." This is a congressional primary-source finding (E1).

**UnionPay Integration:** Russia increasingly depends on China's UnionPay cards, CIPS, and yuan liquidity. Economists warn Russia has traded one dependency (Western financial system) for another (Chinese policy decisions).

### 4. SWIFT Alternatives Assessment

**SPFS (Russia):** Operates only within Russia, high transaction costs, not a viable SWIFT replacement for international trade.

**CIPS (China):** Growing rapidly but still approximately 0.3% the size of SWIFT. Heavy reliance on SWIFT messaging (80%) undermines independence claims.

**INSTEX (EU-Iran):** Confined to humanitarian trade only. Functionally dead as a sanctions bypass mechanism.

**Assessment:** No current alternative replicates SWIFT's network effects. SWIFT disconnection forces Russia into bilateral arrangements (primarily with China) rather than enabling a parallel global system. However, the trend is toward fragmentation -- each sanctions escalation pushes more transaction volume toward CIPS and bilateral currency swaps.

### 5. OFAC Enforcement: What Cases Reveal

**GVA Capital ($215.99M penalty, June 2025):** San Francisco venture capital firm (Cayman Islands-registered) managed $20M investment in Luminar Technologies on behalf of sanctioned Russian oligarch Suleiman Kerimov. Used nephew Nariman Gadzhiev as proxy through dissolved Guernsey entity Prosperity Investments LP. Continued managing investment for 3 years after Kerimov's 2018 designation. Initially produced 173 documents under subpoena, then 1,300 additional records after pre-penalty notice. **Pattern revealed:** Proxy ownership through cascading shell entities in favorable jurisdictions (Guernsey, Cayman Islands).

**Family International Realty ($1.08M, 2024):** Transferred ownership of three luxury NYC condominiums from sanctioned Russian oligarchs to non-sanctioned family members. **Pattern revealed:** Real estate as sanctions evasion vehicle via family transfers.

**Unicat Catalyst Technologies ($3.88M):** Iran and Venezuela sanctions violations. **Pattern revealed:** Industrial supply chain as sanctions conduit.

**Fracht FWO Inc. ($1.61M):** Texas freight forwarder used blocked aircraft and entities for Mexico-to-Argentina transport. **Pattern revealed:** Logistics companies as unwitting/witting intermediaries.

**Analytical observation:** OFAC's total public settlements in 2024 were approximately $50 million -- trivial compared to the scale of evasion ($9 billion in Iranian shadow banking alone through US accounts). This enforcement-to-evasion ratio suggests sanctions enforcement is deliberately calibrated to demonstrate activity without shutting down the system.

### 6. Intermediary Jurisdictions

**UAE:** Most frequently featuring intermediary jurisdiction in Q1 2024. Free trade zones provide corporate opacity. Dubai-based front companies handled $800M+ for Iranian networks. Capital flight destination for Russian wealth post-2022. Maintains Western defense and financial relationships while enabling massive evasion.

**Turkey:** Nine Turkish companies added to BIS Entity List in single October 2025 action -- one of the most sweeping designations ever. Tupras imported record Russian crude (225,000 bpd) in 2024. However, Turkey demonstrated sensitivity to secondary sanctions pressure, shifting procurement away from Russian crude in late 2025. This suggests Turkey's enabling role is opportunistic rather than strategic.

**India:** Functions as the world's largest Russian oil laundering operation through refining. Government position: India is buying oil on the open market at market prices, refining is a sovereign economic activity, and the origin of crude is irrelevant once refined. This is technically correct under existing sanctions architecture, which is precisely the point -- the architecture was designed with this loophole.

**Singapore/Malaysia:** Malaysian waters saw return of "dark fleet" ship-to-ship transfers. Singapore functions as a financial intermediary node. Both maintain Western relationships while enabling transit trade.

### 7. Historical Parallels

**Iraq Oil-for-Food (1996-2003):** The Volcker Commission documented 2,200 companies in 66 countries paying $1.8 billion in kickbacks and surcharges to Saddam Hussein's regime. The program was valued at $60 billion. Suppliers paid 10% kickbacks to be selected as vendors. $228.8 million in oil surcharges, $1.58 billion in humanitarian supply kickbacks. **Key parallel:** The sanctions evasion was not hidden from the UN -- it was enabled by "ineffective, wasteful, and unsatisfactory" management. The evasion was an open secret among participants.

**Apartheid South Africa:** Despite comprehensive sanctions, South Africa continued buying oil on international markets throughout the apartheid era. Iran supplied oil to South Africa until 1979; during the Iran-Iraq war, South Africa supplied weapons to Iran in exchange for oil. South Africa also developed coal-to-liquids technology (Sasol) as a sanctions workaround. **Key parallel:** Sanctions forced technological adaptation and created strange bedfellows (South Africa-Iran), but did not prevent access to critical commodities.

**Common pattern:** In both historical cases, sanctions evasion was widespread, well-documented during the sanctions period itself, and tolerated by the international community because the alternative (actual enforcement) would have disrupted trade relationships that benefited sanctions-imposing nations.

### 8. The Central Analytical Question: Real Tool or Managed Theater?

The evidence supports a dual-nature assessment:

**Sanctions ARE real** in that they: (a) impose 15-30% transaction cost premiums on sanctioned trade, (b) force Russia into Chinese financial dependency, (c) disrupt specific entities when targeted (Litasco dismantled, Tupras forced to shift sourcing), (d) create legal risk that deters risk-averse Western institutions.

**Sanctions ARE theater** in that they: (a) rely on attestation systems designed not to be verified, (b) permit $14.3 billion in Indian re-export of Russian-origin fuel to Europe, (c) tolerate $9 billion in Iranian shadow banking through US correspondent accounts, (d) enforce at a scale ($50M in penalties) trivial compared to evasion volume, (e) create intermediary profit opportunities that incentivize the very evasion they nominally prohibit.

**Assessment:** Sanctions are a managed friction system, not a blockade. They redistribute profits (from direct traders to intermediaries), alter supply chain geography (from direct to triangular trade), and create political narrative (Western governments "acting" against adversaries) while maintaining commodity flows that the global economy requires. This is structurally identical to the historical pattern: Oil-for-Food, apartheid sanctions, and current regimes all exhibit the same enforcement-evasion equilibrium. Confidence in "managed friction" characterization: 0.70-0.80.

## Negative Findings

1. **INSTEX is functionally dead.** The EU's Instrument for Supporting Trade Exchanges, designed as a SWIFT alternative for EU-Iran trade, is confined to humanitarian transactions and has processed negligible volume. It is not a meaningful sanctions evasion tool and should not be characterized as one. (E1, 0.90)

2. **CIPS is NOT yet an independent parallel to SWIFT.** Despite rapid growth, 80% of CIPS payments still route through SWIFT messaging. CIPS is a settlement system, not an independent messaging network. Claims that CIPS enables full sanctions evasion are overstated. (E2, 0.75-0.80)

3. **SPFS has no international viability.** Russia's domestic payment messaging system operates only within Russia and has high transaction costs. It is not a SWIFT alternative for international trade. (E2, 0.80)

4. **No evidence of centrally coordinated Russia-Iran-China sanctions evasion cartel.** While all three countries benefit from and facilitate evasion, the evidence shows parallel opportunistic systems rather than a unified coordinated architecture. China facilitates all three (including DPRK), but the relationships are bilateral, not trilateral. The USCC characterizes Chinese facilitation as state policy, but this is Chinese policy serving Chinese interests, not cartel coordination. (E3, 0.60-0.70)

5. **Turkey's intermediary role appears opportunistic, not strategic.** Tupras's rapid shift away from Russian crude after January 2025 US sanctions suggests Turkish refining of Russian crude was profit-driven rather than geopolitically motivated. Turkey responded to secondary sanctions pressure in months, not years. (E1, 0.80-0.85)

6. **The $60 oil price cap was NOT designed to fail -- but its enforcement mechanism was inadequate by design.** The attestation system was created knowing it could not be systematically verified. This is distinct from deliberate sabotage; it reflects the political constraint of needing to maintain oil supply while appearing to sanction Russia. (E3, 0.65-0.75)

7. **No evidence that Swiss commodity traders (Trafigura, Vitol, Glencore) are currently violating sanctions.** They exited direct Russian crude trading. The question of whether their infrastructure was repurposed by successors is unresolved but not documented. (E3, 0.50-0.60)

## Analytical Notes

### On the "Managed Theater" Hypothesis
This report's central finding -- that sanctions function as managed friction rather than blockade -- should not be interpreted as claiming sanctions have zero effect. They demonstrably do: Russia's financial isolation from SWIFT forced dependency on Chinese systems; specific entities (Litasco, GVA Capital) faced real consequences; Turkey's refining behavior changed under secondary sanctions pressure. The finding is that the aggregate system is calibrated to permit continued commodity flows while imposing costs. The enforcement-to-evasion ratio ($50M vs $9B+ in a single category) is the clearest quantitative indicator of this calibration.

### On Evidence Tier Discipline
Maritime tracking data (Windward, Kpler) is classified E2 rather than E1 because it is proprietary analytical output, not raw data or government records. However, its reliability is high -- these firms provide data to governments and enforcement agencies. The OFAC, FinCEN, USCC, and EU sources are classified E1 as official government documents. The "managed friction" synthesis (XFN-099) is classified E3 as analytical inference, despite being derived from multiple E1 sources, because the characterization is the analyst's own assessment rather than a documented finding.

### Cross-Campaign Integration Priority
The intersection of sanctions evasion infrastructure with energy trader networks (XFIN P2) and specific country financial systems (PKG-IRN P5, PKG-RUS P7, PKG-CHN P4) should be the focus of the XFIN MOSAIC synthesis phase. The key question for synthesis: does the sanctions evasion infrastructure map onto the same intermediary networks identified in the Epstein-BCCI-Bear Stearns investigation (PKG-EP), or is it a distinct system? The jurisdictional overlap (UAE, Cayman Islands, Guernsey, Switzerland, Singapore) is suggestive but not yet documented at the operational level.
