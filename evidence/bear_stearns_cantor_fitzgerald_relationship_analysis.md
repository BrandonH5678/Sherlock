## 1. STRUCTURAL MARKET RELATIONSHIP

### 1.1 Different Roles in the Same Market

**Bear Stearns** was a **primary dealer** -- one of approximately 20-25 firms designated by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York to trade U.S. government securities directly with the Fed. Primary dealers are required to participate in Treasury auctions and make markets in government securities.

**Cantor Fitzgerald** was an **interdealer broker (IDB)** -- the intermediary that facilitated trades between primary dealers. Founded in 1945, Cantor became the world's first electronic marketplace for U.S. government securities in 1972 and by 2001 handled approximately 25% of all daily Treasury bond transactions (some sources cite 70% of all Treasury securities traded through the firm).

**Critical distinction:** Cantor Fitzgerald did NOT become a primary dealer itself until **August 1, 2006** (per Federal Reserve Bank of New York records). Before that date, Cantor operated exclusively as an IDB -- meaning firms like Bear Stearns were its **customers**, not its competitors.

**[E1 | Confidence: 0.95]** Primary dealer designation dates are Federal Reserve public records. Market structure roles are documented in academic and regulatory sources.

Sources:

- [Bear Stearns 10-K Filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/777001/000116923206000651/d66710_10-k.htm)

### 1.2 Implied Trading Relationship

Since nearly all interdealer trades in U.S. Treasury securities were conducted through IDBs, and Cantor was the dominant IDB handling 25-70% of this volume, Bear Stearns (as a primary dealer) would have routinely executed Treasury trades through Cantor Fitzgerald's platform. This is not speculation -- it is the structural reality of how the government securities market operated.

**Assessment:** Their trading relationship was **inherent to the market structure** -- comparable to a stock exchange and its listed companies. Every primary dealer used IDB services; Cantor was the largest provider. Bear Stearns was almost certainly one of Cantor's most important clients.

**[E2 | Confidence: 0.85]** No specific documentation of Bear Stearns as a Cantor client has been located, but the market structure makes it near-certain.

### 1.3 eSpeed Electronic Trading Platform

Cantor's eSpeed platform, launched in 1996 and released publicly in March 1999, was the dominant electronic trading system for U.S. Treasury securities. Bear Stearns is documented as an early participant in electronic trading platforms in the fixed-income space. One source mentions Bear Stearns as among the first participants in MarketAxess alongside Deutsche Bank and J.P. Morgan.

While no specific documentation confirms Bear Stearns used eSpeed, given eSpeed's dominance in Treasury trading and Bear's status as a primary dealer, usage is highly probable.

## 2. CLEARING AND SETTLEMENT

### 2.1 Bear Stearns as Major Clearing Firm

Bear Stearns Securities Corp. (BSSC) was the **third-largest clearing firm** on Wall Street, with 275 broker-dealer clients. BSSC offered global clearing services to broker-dealers, prime broker clients, and other professional traders, including securities lending.

### 2.2 Cantor Fitzgerald's Clearing Operations

Cantor Fitzgerald did not launch its own clearing operation ("Cantor Clearing") until **2012** -- four years after Bear Stearns' collapse. Before that, Cantor relied on external clearing firms for settlement.

**Assessment:** It is plausible (but unconfirmed) that Bear Stearns cleared some of Cantor's trades before 2008. Bear was a major clearing firm; Cantor was a major IDB. The relationship would have been normal industry practice.

**[E3 | Confidence: 0.45]** No documentation found of specific clearing relationship. The 2012 launch of Cantor Clearing suggests Cantor previously used external clearing services.

### 2.3 GSCC/FICC Membership

Both firms would have been members of the Government Securities Clearing Corporation (GSCC), which merged with MBS Clearing Corporation in 2003 to form the Fixed Income Clearing Corporation (FICC), a subsidiary of DTCC. As members of the same clearing infrastructure, their trades would have been netted and settled through this shared system.

**[E1 | Confidence: 0.90]** GSCC/FICC membership is standard for government securities dealers and IDBs. Board representation included primary dealers and clearing banks.
