## Executive Summary

Russia's energy sector operates as the primary mechanism of state power projection, with Gazprom and Rosneft functioning not as commercial enterprises but as instruments of Kremlin strategic policy. The sector's relationship with Western finance reveals a three-decade arc: initial integration (1990s-2006), deepening entanglement despite warning signs (2006-2014), partial decoupling under sanctions (2014-2022), and forced separation with massive evasion infrastructure (2022-present). The Yukos seizure (2003-2006) established the template — private energy assets are held at the pleasure of the state, and Western banks will facilitate access to stolen assets if the fees are sufficient. The Rosneft 2006 IPO, underwritten by ABN AMRO Rothschild, Dresdner Kleinwort, JP Morgan, and Morgan Stanley, legitimized assets stripped from Yukos through politically motivated prosecution — a fact documented by the Permanent Court of Arbitration's $50 billion judgment. The Lukoil executive death pattern (4 senior figures, 2022-2024) correlates with the company's unprecedented public call for peace in Ukraine, though direct attribution remains contested. Igor Sechin emerges as Russia's most powerful energy figure — probable former KGB, siloviki faction leader, architect of the Yukos seizure, and the man who transformed Rosneft from a minor player into the world's largest publicly traded oil company using assets acquired through state expropriation. Gerhard Schröder's trajectory from German Chancellor to Nord Stream/Rosneft board member within weeks of leaving office represents one of the most documented cases of Western political capture by Russian energy interests. Post-2022, a shadow fleet of 600-1,400 vessels, built at ~$10 billion cost, now carries 70% of Russian seaborne oil exports outside Western insurance and tracking systems.

## Key Findings

### 1. Gazprom: State Control Architecture

Gazprom is controlled by the Russian Federation through a 50.23% stake held via three entities: the Federal Agency for State Property Management (38.37%), Rosneftegaz (10.97%), and Rosgazifikatsiya (0.89%). Board Chairman Viktor Zubkov is a former Prime Minister of Russia. CEO Alexey Miller was installed by Putin in 2001 as a trusted former colleague from the St. Petersburg mayor's office. The board is populated with security services alumni and Putin associates. Gazprom posted a net loss of 629 billion rubles ($6.9 billion) in 2023 — its first annual loss in over 20 years — as European gas sales collapsed 55.6% to 28.3 bcm and Russia's share of European gas imports fell from 40% (2021) to 8% (2023). This collapse followed Nord Stream pipeline sabotage and European political decisions to diversify supply.

### 2. Rosneft: The Yukos Inheritance

Rosneft's rise was built on the expropriation of Yukos. In December 2004, Yukos' most valuable subsidiary, Yuganskneftegaz (worth up to $20 billion), was sold to an obscure shell company with 10,000 rubles ($300) in capital for $9.3 billion. Days later, Rosneft purchased this shell for 100 rubles. The Permanent Court of Arbitration ruled in July 2014 that Russia had expropriated Yukos through "politically motivated attacks" and that Russian courts "bent to the will of Russian executive authorities." The arbitral award: $50 billion. The ECHR separately awarded EUR 1.87 billion. Rosneft is currently ~75% state-owned: Rosneftegaz (40.4%), BP Russian Investments (19.75% — announced divestiture Feb 2022, still unwinding), and QH Oil Investments/Qatar (18.46%).

### 3. The Rosneft IPO: Western Banks Legitimize Expropriation

In July 2006, Rosneft completed a $10.7 billion IPO — the largest in Russian history and fifth-largest worldwide — on the London Stock Exchange, RTS, and MICEX. The joint global coordinators/bookrunners were ABN AMRO Rothschild, Dresdner Kleinwort, JP Morgan, and Morgan Stanley. Strategic investors included BP ($1 billion), Petronas ($1.5 billion), and CNPC ($0.5 billion). The IPO was designed to repay loans to Western banks. Western investment banks thus directly profited from laundering expropriated Yukos assets into legitimate international capital markets — a fact that was publicly known at the time but did not prevent participation.

### 4. The 2016 Rosneft Privatization: Matryoshka Opacity

In December 2016, a 19.5% Rosneft stake was sold for EUR 10.2 billion ($11 billion) to a consortium of Glencore and Qatar's sovereign wealth fund — the largest foreign investment in Russia since the Ukraine crisis began. The ownership chain ran through QHG Shares Pte Ltd (Singapore) → QHG Investment LLP (UK) → QHG Holding (UK) → Glencore Energy UK + Qatar Holding + QHG Cayman Ltd (Cayman Islands). This "matryoshka" structure — shell companies owning shell companies across multiple jurisdictions — obscured beneficial ownership. The Cayman entity's ownership remains opaque.

### 5. Lukoil: The Peace Statement and Executive Deaths

Lukoil, Russia's largest private oil company, became the only major Russian company to publicly call for "the soonest termination of the armed conflict" in Ukraine in March 2022. The subsequent executive death timeline:
- **May 2022:** Alexander Subbotin (former top manager) — found dead at a shaman's house
- **September 2022:** Ravil Maganov (chairman) — died after "falling from a hospital window." Lukoil's statement cited "severe illness" with no mention of a fall. Two associates said they believed suicide was "highly unlikely."
- **October 2023:** Vladimir Nekrasov (chairman, Maganov's replacement) — died suddenly, aged 66, of "acute heart failure"
- **March 2024:** Vitaly Robertus (vice president) — died suddenly, reported as suicide

Founder Vagit Alekperov resigned April 21, 2022, days after UK/Australia sanctions. His resignation was characterized as protective — removing a sanctioned individual from leadership to preserve company operations.

### 6. Igor Sechin: The Siloviki Godfather

Sechin (b. 1960) is consistently described as Russia's second most powerful person. Key biographical elements:
- **1984:** Graduated Leningrad State University as a linguist (Portuguese, French)
- **1980s:** Served in Mozambique and Angola as "interpreter" with Soviet missions — widely assessed as KGB cover assignment given the operational context of Soviet activities in southern Africa during Cold War proxy conflicts
- **Early 1990s:** Became Putin's chief of staff when Putin was deputy mayor of St. Petersburg
- **2000:** Deputy chief of staff in Putin's presidential administration, overseeing security services and energy
- **2004:** Appointed Rosneft board chairman; orchestrated Yukos seizure and asset transfer
- **2008-2012:** Deputy Prime Minister
- **2012-present:** Rosneft CEO/President

Family network: Daughter Inga married Dmitry Ustinov (son of Justice Minister Vladimir Ustinov; Dmitry is an FSB Academy graduate), later married Timerbulat Karimov (senior VP at VTB Bank). This marriage pattern — security services + state banking — exemplifies siloviki dynasty formation.

Sechin's yacht Amore Vero ($120M, formerly "St. Princess Olga" — renamed after 2017 divorce from Olga Sechina) was seized by French authorities in March 2022. A second yacht, Crescent, was seized by Spanish/Italian authorities.

### 7. Nord Stream: Western Capital Builds Russian Leverage

**Nord Stream 1:** Gazprom 51%, Wintershall Holding (BASF) 15.5%, E.ON 15.5%, Gasunie (Netherlands) 9%, Engie (France) 9%.

**Nord Stream 2:** Gazprom sole shareholder of Nord Stream 2 AG. Five European companies each committed EUR 950 million (total EUR 4.75 billion = 50% of the EUR 9.5 billion project cost): ENGIE, OMV, Shell, Uniper, and Wintershall. Post-2022, all five exited: Wintershall Dea wrote off ~EUR 1 billion; Uniper recognized EUR 987 million impairment.

### 8. Gerhard Schröder: Chancellor-to-Boardroom Pipeline

- **November 2005:** In his final weeks as Chancellor, signed the North European Gas Pipeline (Nord Stream 1) deal with Gazprom
- **December 2005:** Within days of leaving office, joined Nord Stream AG board as chairman
- **2017:** Elected head of Rosneft supervisory board at Russian government's suggestion
- **2022:** Resigned Nord Stream chairmanship after Russia's invasion of Ukraine; dropped planned Gazprom board seat
- **Compensation:** ~$270,000/year as Nord Stream shareholder committee chairman (reported 2022)
- Putin called Schröder a "flawless democrat" (2004); Schröder's friendship with Putin is personal and longstanding

### 9. Sanctions Evasion: The Shadow Fleet

Post-2022, Russia built a shadow fleet of 600-1,400 vessels (estimates vary; ~400 crude tankers confirmed) at approximately $10 billion investment. This fleet carries 70% of Russian seaborne oil and 89% of crude oil exports. Evasion mechanisms:
- **Flag manipulation:** Registration under Panama, Liberia, Marshall Islands, Cook Islands, Gabon
- **AIS manipulation:** Transponders disabled ("going dark") to avoid satellite tracking
- **Ship-to-ship transfers:** Open-sea cargo transfers to obscure origin
- **Falsified certificates of origin:** Russian crude relabeled as non-Russian
- **Blending:** Urals crude mixed with other grades and renamed
- **Insurance opacity:** Two-thirds of shadow fleet vessels have "unknown" insurers; state-backed Russian reinsurance replaces Western P&I clubs

India ramped Russian crude imports to 1.6-1.75 million bpd by 2023 — a 1,000% increase from pre-war levels. Between December 2022 and December 2023, price cap coalition members imported ~$9 billion of Russian-origin oil products from India and other third countries.

### 10. Western Trading Houses: Swiss Nexus

Before 2022, 50-60% of Russian crude was traded from Switzerland. Vitol, Trafigura, Glencore, and Gunvor handled over 1 million bpd of Russian oil — one-eighth of daily crude sales. Gunvor was co-founded by Putin ally Gennady Timchenko. All four companies officially ceased Russian oil trading after 2022 sanctions, but replacement trading firms — smaller, privately held, operating from Dubai and Fujairah — filled the gap. The major traders have publicly stated readiness to resume Russian trading if sanctions are eased.

### 11. Dynastic Succession Challenges

Russian oligarch dynasties face a structural problem absent in Western dynasties: wealth rests on informal relationships with the Kremlin that are difficult to transfer intergenerationally. Approximately 70% of ultra-wealthy Russians consider "wealth succession" among their biggest problems. Examples of energy sector succession attempts:
- Leonid Fedun transferred ~$1.4 billion in Lukoil holdings to son Anton and daughter Ekaterina
- "Kremlin kids" increasingly run strategic state companies and win state contracts
- Alekperov himself stated: "This work is too challenging to bequeath to my son"
- Sechin's family operates through marriage alliances to security services and state banking rather than direct corporate succession

## Negative Findings

1. **No direct evidence of coordinated Western government complicity in Rosneft IPO.** While the IPO legitimized expropriated assets, and this was publicly known, the decision to participate was made by individual investment banks for commercial reasons. No evidence of government directives to facilitate the IPO has been found. The UK listing authority's decision to approve the LSE listing despite Yukos provenance is a regulatory question, not evidence of conspiracy. **NEGATIVE FINDING (E1 absence).**

2. **Litvinenko assassination — no documented Lukoil connection.** While Litvinenko's assassination (Nov 2006) involved Russian state actors and occurred during a period of energy sector consolidation, no evidence connects it to Lukoil corporate espionage or internal conflicts. The Lukoil executive deaths (2022-2024) are a separate pattern. **NEGATIVE FINDING.**

3. **Lukoil executive deaths — attribution remains unproven.** While the correlation between the March 2022 peace statement and subsequent deaths is striking, no direct evidence of state-ordered assassination has been published. Alternative explanations (genuine illness, suicide under pressure, coincidence) cannot be excluded. The pattern is suggestive but not conclusive. **Assign E2/E3 for state attribution; E1 only for the deaths themselves.**

4. **Sechin's KGB service — never officially confirmed.** While his 1980s Angola/Mozambique service is documented and the KGB cover assessment is widely shared by analysts, no official KGB records or direct confirmation exists in the public domain. **E2 (strong circumstantial) but not E1.**

5. **No evidence of Gazprom board members resisting Kremlin directives.** The board functions as a transmission mechanism for state policy, not as an independent governance body. However, no leaked internal documents or whistleblower testimony documenting specific instances of board members being ordered to vote a particular way has surfaced. The control is structural rather than documented through specific directives. **Structural inference (E2).**

6. **Schröder as "agent of influence" — no evidence of recruitment.** While Schröder's behavior is consistent with influence operations (sign deal → immediate board appointment → decades of advocacy), no evidence of formal intelligence recruitment, covert payments beyond disclosed compensation, or blackmail leverage has been published. The relationship appears transactional rather than coercive. **NEGATIVE FINDING for intelligence recruitment; E1 for financial relationship.**

7. **Dynastic succession in Russian energy — no clear second-generation CEO-level takeover documented.** Unlike Western dynasties where succession is often formalized, Russian energy sector succession remains informal and personality-dependent. The Fedun-to-children Lukoil transfer is a wealth transfer, not a management succession. **NEGATIVE FINDING for operational succession.**
